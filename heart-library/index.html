The heart library

Plain-language guides to heart conditions, tests and treatments — written to help you understand your reports and ask better questions.

Heart attack warning signs

  • Chest pain, pressure or tightness lasting more than a few minutes, or coming back
  • Pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck, back or upper stomach
  • Sweating, breathlessness, nausea or sudden weakness
  • Women, older people and people with diabetes may feel only breathlessness, tiredness or discomfort

What to do

  1. Call an ambulance — don't drive yourself, and don't wait to see if it passes.
  2. Ask for a hospital with a 24×7 cath lab for primary angioplasty.
  3. If you're not allergic and have no bleeding problem, chew one aspirin (300 mg) while you wait — follow the emergency team's advice.
  4. Sit down, loosen tight clothing and keep someone with you.

Heart conditions

Angina

Chest discomfort that comes on with effort or stress and settles with rest — a warning that the heart muscle is short of blood.

What you may notice

  • Pressure, tightness or heaviness in the chest
  • Discomfort spreading to the arm, neck, jaw or back
  • Breathlessness or tiredness on walking uphill or climbing stairs
  • Symptoms that ease within a few minutes of rest

How it is checked

  • ECG and echocardiogram
  • Treadmill test or stress imaging
  • CT coronary angiogram or coronary angiography when needed

Treatment

  • Medicines to relieve symptoms and protect the arteries
  • Controlling blood pressure, cholesterol, sugar and tobacco
  • Angioplasty and stenting, or bypass surgery, for selected patients

Get help now: Chest pain at rest, pain lasting more than 10–15 minutes, or angina that is suddenly worse or more frequent can be a heart attack. Call 112 or 108.

Heart attack (myocardial infarction)

A coronary artery becomes blocked, usually by a clot on a cholesterol plaque, and heart muscle starts to die. Every minute counts.

Warning signs

  • Chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes
  • Pain in the arm, jaw, neck, back or upper abdomen
  • Sweating, nausea, breathlessness, light-headedness
  • Women, older people and people with diabetes may have milder or unusual symptoms

What happens in hospital

  • An ECG within minutes of arrival
  • Opening the artery as fast as possible — ideally primary angioplasty in a 24×7 cath lab
  • Clot-dissolving medicine where angioplasty isn't quickly available

After a heart attack

  • Medicines that lower the chance of another event
  • Cardiac rehabilitation and a gradual return to activity
  • Stopping tobacco completely and tight risk-factor control

Get help now: Do not drive yourself. Call 112 or 108 and ask to be taken to a hospital with a cath lab. If you are not allergic to aspirin and have no bleeding problem, chewing one aspirin (300 mg) while help comes is commonly advised — follow the emergency team's instructions.

Coronary artery disease

Cholesterol-rich plaque builds up inside the arteries that feed the heart. It is the most common cause of angina and heart attacks — and it starts earlier in Indians.

Who is at risk

  • Diabetes, high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol or Lp(a)
  • Smoking, chewing tobacco or gutkha
  • A family history of early heart disease
  • Inactivity, central obesity, poor sleep and stress

How it is found

  • Risk assessment and blood tests
  • CT calcium score or CT coronary angiogram
  • Stress testing; coronary angiography with imaging (IVUS/OCT) or pressure-wire (FFR) when deciding on treatment

Treatment

  • Lifestyle and medicines first — they reduce events substantially
  • Angioplasty or bypass when symptoms persist or the anatomy needs it

High blood pressure (hypertension)

Pressure in the arteries stays too high. It rarely causes symptoms but steadily damages the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes.

Numbers to know

  • Clinic readings of 140/90 or above on repeated checks mean hypertension
  • Home readings of 135/85 or above count as high
  • 120–139 / 70–89 is 'elevated' — worth acting on

What helps

  • Less salt (watch pickles, papad, namkeen and restaurant food)
  • Regular brisk walking, weight and waist control
  • Limiting alcohol and stopping tobacco
  • Medicines — usually a combination at low doses — taken every day

Monitoring

  • A validated upper-arm home monitor
  • 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) to confirm the diagnosis or check treatment

Get help now: A reading of 180/110 or more with chest pain, breathlessness, severe headache, weakness or vision change needs emergency care.

High cholesterol and lipids

LDL ('bad') cholesterol is the main fuel for artery plaque. Lower is better, and your target depends on your overall risk.

What the report shows

  • LDL cholesterol — the number most treatment decisions are based on
  • Non-HDL cholesterol and triglycerides
  • HDL — a higher HDL does not cancel out a high LDL
  • Lipoprotein(a) — inherited; worth checking once in life

Typical LDL targets

  • After a heart attack, stent or stroke: below 55 mg/dL (some patients lower)
  • Diabetes with other risks: below 70 mg/dL
  • Others: set from your risk score

Treatment

  • Diet and activity
  • Statins first, with ezetimibe, bempedoic acid or PCSK9-targeted therapy when needed

Lipoprotein(a) — Lp(a)

An inherited cholesterol particle that raises the risk of heart attack and aortic valve narrowing. Common in South Asians and not changed by diet.

Who should test

  • Everyone, once in adult life
  • Anyone with early heart disease in themselves or the family
  • People whose LDL is controlled but who still have events

If it is high

  • Your other risk factors are treated more strictly
  • LDL targets are set lower
  • Family members are advised to check theirs

Heart failure

The heart cannot pump or fill well enough for the body's needs. Modern treatment helps people live longer and feel much better.

What you may notice

  • Breathlessness on effort or when lying flat
  • Swollen ankles and rapid weight gain
  • Tiredness and reduced exercise capacity

How it is checked

  • Echocardiogram to measure pumping (ejection fraction)
  • NT-proBNP blood test, ECG and chest X-ray
  • Search for the cause — blocked arteries, valve disease, high BP, rhythm problems

Treatment

  • The four pillars of medicines for reduced pumping
  • Salt and fluid advice, daily weights
  • Devices — CRT or ICD — for selected patients
  • Treating the underlying cause

Get help now: Severe breathlessness at rest or when lying down, or coughing pink frothy sputum, needs emergency care.

Atrial fibrillation (AF)

An irregular, often fast heartbeat from the upper chambers. It raises the risk of stroke, which is largely preventable.

What you may notice

  • Palpitations or an irregular pulse
  • Breathlessness, tiredness or dizziness
  • Sometimes no symptoms — found on an ECG or smartwatch

Treatment

  • Blood thinners to prevent stroke, based on a risk score
  • Rate or rhythm control — medicines, cardioversion or ablation
  • Managing blood pressure, weight, sleep apnoea and alcohol

Palpitations and SVT

An awareness of the heartbeat — fast, pounding or skipping. Most causes are harmless, but some rhythms need treatment.

Common causes

  • Extra beats (ectopics), caffeine, stress, poor sleep
  • SVT — sudden runs of a very fast regular beat that start and stop abruptly
  • Atrial fibrillation, thyroid problems, anaemia

How it is checked

  • ECG during symptoms (a smartwatch strip helps)
  • Holter monitoring or an event loop recorder
  • Echocardiogram

Treatment

  • Reassurance when it's benign
  • Vagal manoeuvres and medicines for SVT
  • Catheter ablation — often a permanent cure for SVT

Get help now: Palpitations with fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness need urgent assessment.

Fainting (syncope)

A brief loss of consciousness from a drop in blood flow to the brain. Usually benign, but it must be properly assessed.

Red flags

  • Fainting during exercise or while lying down
  • Fainting with palpitations or chest pain
  • A family history of sudden death at a young age
  • Known heart disease

How it is checked

  • ECG, echocardiogram and blood tests
  • Holter, event loop recorder or tilt test

Heart valve disease

A valve becomes narrow (stenosis) or leaky (regurgitation). Rheumatic heart disease is still an important cause in India.

What you may notice

  • Breathlessness, tiredness, chest pain or fainting on effort
  • A murmur heard by your doctor

Treatment

  • Regular echocardiograms to time treatment well
  • Balloon valvuloplasty for suitable mitral stenosis
  • Surgical or catheter valve replacement (TAVI) when needed

Cardiomyopathy

A disease of the heart muscle itself — dilated, thickened (hypertrophic) or stiff. Some types run in families.

How it is checked

  • Echocardiogram and cardiac MRI
  • Holter monitoring for rhythm risk
  • Genetic counselling and family screening where relevant

Treatment

  • Heart failure medicines
  • ICD for people at risk of dangerous rhythms
  • Specific therapies for certain types

Peripheral artery disease

Narrowed arteries to the legs. Calf pain on walking is the classic sign — and it signals a higher risk of heart attack and stroke.

What you may notice

  • Cramping calf, thigh or buttock pain on walking that eases with rest
  • Cold feet, slow-healing wounds
  • In diabetes, symptoms may be minimal

Tests and treatment

  • Ankle-brachial index (ABI), Doppler and CT angiography
  • Supervised walking, stopping tobacco, statins and antiplatelets
  • Peripheral angioplasty or surgery for severe or limb-threatening disease

Get help now: A suddenly cold, pale, painful or numb leg is an emergency.

Carotid disease and stroke

Plaque in the neck arteries can cause stroke. Narrowing found early can be treated to lower that risk.

Warning signs of stroke — act FAST

  • Face drooping
  • Arm or leg weakness
  • Speech difficulty
  • Time to call 112 — clot treatments work best in the first hours

Treatment

  • Medicines and risk-factor control for everyone
  • Carotid stenting or surgery for selected significant narrowings

Get help now: Any sudden weakness, numbness, speech or vision loss — call 112 immediately, even if it improves.

Aortic aneurysm and dissection

The main artery from the heart can balloon (aneurysm) or tear (dissection). Aneurysms are usually silent until found on a scan.

Who should be checked

  • Men over 65 who have smoked
  • People with a family history of aortic disease
  • Long-standing high blood pressure

Treatment

  • Strict blood pressure control and surveillance scans
  • Endovascular repair (EVAR/TEVAR) with stent grafts, or open surgery

Get help now: Sudden severe tearing pain in the chest or back is an emergency — call 112.

Congenital heart disease

Heart differences present from birth — such as holes between chambers (ASD, VSD) or a persistent duct (PDA). Many can be closed without surgery.

Treatment

  • Device closure through a catheter for suitable ASD, VSD and PDA
  • Balloon treatment for narrowed valves or coarctation
  • Lifelong follow-up for adults who had surgery as children

Pericarditis

Inflammation of the sac around the heart — sharp chest pain that is worse lying flat and better sitting forward.

Treatment

  • Anti-inflammatory medicines and colchicine
  • Echocardiogram to check for fluid
  • Rest from strenuous exercise until settled

Tests

ECG (electrocardiogram)

A two-minute recording of the heart's electrical activity using stickers on the chest, arms and legs. Painless and quick.

It can show

  • Heart attacks — current or old
  • Rhythm problems and conduction blocks
  • Thickened heart muscle and electrolyte effects

Good to know

  • A normal resting ECG does not rule out blocked arteries
  • Bring old ECGs — comparison is very useful

Echocardiogram (echo)

An ultrasound scan of the heart. It shows the pumping strength, the valves, the heart walls and fluid around the heart.

What to expect

  • About 20–30 minutes lying on your left side
  • Gel and a probe on the chest; no radiation
  • Transoesophageal echo (TEE) is a special version through the food pipe for detailed valve views

Treadmill test (TMT / stress test)

An ECG recorded while you walk on a treadmill at increasing speed and slope, to see how the heart responds to effort.

Before the test

  • Wear comfortable shoes
  • Have a light meal two hours before
  • Ask whether to pause any medicines

Good to know

  • Stress echo or nuclear scans are more accurate in some people
  • Not suitable for everyone — your doctor decides

Holter monitoring

A small recorder worn for 24–48 hours (or longer) that captures every heartbeat while you go about your day.

Used for

  • Palpitations, dizziness or fainting
  • Checking rhythm after a heart attack or in cardiomyopathy
  • Assessing how well rhythm treatment is working

Tips

  • Keep a short diary of symptoms and activities
  • Avoid bathing while it's on

24-hour blood pressure monitoring (ABPM)

An arm cuff that measures your blood pressure every 20–30 minutes for a day and night.

Used for

  • Confirming hypertension and spotting 'white-coat' readings
  • Finding high BP at night
  • Checking treatment over 24 hours

Event loop recorder (ELR)

A monitor worn for one to several weeks that records the heart rhythm when symptoms occur — for symptoms that come and go.

Used for

  • Infrequent palpitations
  • Unexplained fainting
  • Looking for hidden AF after a stroke

CT coronary angiogram and calcium score

A CT scan that shows the coronary arteries without a catheter. The calcium score measures hard plaque and helps judge long-term risk.

Good to know

  • Needs a steady, slower heart rate — sometimes a tablet beforehand
  • Uses contrast (dye) for the angiogram; calcium score needs none
  • Excellent at ruling out significant blockages

Coronary angiography

A thin tube is passed from the wrist (or groin) to the heart and dye is injected to film the coronary arteries. It is the definitive test for blockages.

What to expect

  • Local anaesthetic; you are awake
  • Usually 20–30 minutes; most people go home the same or next day
  • Wrist (radial) access means walking around soon after

Extra tools

  • IVUS or OCT imaging from inside the artery
  • Pressure-wire (FFR/iFR) to decide whether a narrowing truly needs a stent

Treatments & procedures

Angioplasty and stents (PCI)

A balloon opens a narrowed coronary artery and a drug-eluting stent keeps it open. Done through the wrist in most patients.

When it's used

  • During a heart attack — the fastest way to restore blood flow
  • Angina not controlled by medicines
  • High-risk blockages such as the left main artery

After a stent

  • Antiplatelet medicines exactly as prescribed — never stop them without asking
  • Statins and risk-factor control for life
  • Most people return to desk work within a week

Complex angioplasty

Specialised techniques for difficult blockages that once needed bypass surgery.

Includes

  • Chronic total occlusions (CTO) — arteries blocked for months
  • Left main and bifurcation stenting
  • Heavily calcified arteries — rotational atherectomy and lithotripsy
  • Imaging-guided stenting with IVUS/OCT

Bypass surgery (CABG)

A surgeon uses arteries or veins to route blood around blocked coronary arteries. Best for some patterns of multi-vessel disease, especially with diabetes.

Good to know

  • The choice between stents and bypass is made with a heart team
  • A second opinion on your angiogram can help you decide with confidence

Pacemakers

A small device under the skin that keeps the heart from beating too slowly.

When it's needed

  • Heart block
  • Sick sinus syndrome with slow heart rates and symptoms

Living with one

  • Day-care or overnight procedure
  • Avoid heavy lifting with that arm for a few weeks
  • Mobile phones and household appliances are safe; keep phones off the device pocket

ICD and CRT devices

An ICD watches for dangerous rhythms and corrects them with pacing or a shock. CRT (biventricular pacing) helps a weak heart beat in step.

Who benefits

  • People who survived a cardiac arrest
  • Weak heart muscle with high rhythm risk
  • Heart failure with a wide QRS on ECG (CRT)

Catheter ablation

Catheters find and treat the small area of heart tissue causing an abnormal rhythm.

Used for

  • SVT and WPW — often curative
  • Atrial flutter and atrial fibrillation
  • Some ventricular rhythms

Structural heart procedures

Catheter treatments for valves and holes in the heart, without opening the chest.

Includes

  • Balloon mitral valvuloplasty
  • Device closure of ASD, VSD and PDA
  • Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) — assessed with a heart team

Carotid, peripheral and aortic procedures

Endovascular treatment for arteries outside the heart.

Includes

  • Carotid angioplasty and stenting
  • Leg (peripheral) and kidney (renal) artery angioplasty
  • Stent-graft repair of aortic aneurysm and dissection (EVAR/TEVAR)

Living well

Exercise and your heart

Regular activity lowers blood pressure, sugar and weight and improves mood. The best exercise is the one you'll keep doing.

How much

  • 150 minutes a week of brisk walking (you can talk, not sing) — or 75 minutes of vigorous activity
  • Muscle-strengthening twice a week
  • Break up long sitting every 30–60 minutes

Do it safely

  • Start slowly and build up over weeks
  • In Delhi summers walk early or late, and drink water
  • Get checked first if you have symptoms, known heart disease, or are starting intense training after 40

Get help now: Stop and seek help if exercise brings on chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or palpitations.

Eating for a healthy heart — the Indian plate

Small, steady changes to everyday Indian food make a real difference.

Eat more

  • Vegetables, dals, chana, rajma, sprouts
  • Whole grains — millets (bajra, jowar, ragi), brown rice, atta with bran
  • Nuts and seeds in small handfuls; fruit instead of juice

Eat less

  • Salt — pickles, papad, namkeen, sauces, packaged food
  • Refined flour, sweets and sugary drinks (including 'healthy' juices)
  • Deep-fried food and reused frying oil; vanaspati and trans fats
  • Red and processed meat

Tobacco — smoking, chewing and vaping

Every form of tobacco damages arteries, including gutkha, khaini, bidis and hookah. Stopping is the single most powerful step you can take.

What helps

  • A quit date and telling family and friends
  • Nicotine replacement or prescribed medicines
  • India's free quitline: 1800-11-2356

Alcohol and the heart

Current evidence does not support drinking for heart health. Less is better, and none is best.

Why it matters

  • Raises blood pressure and triglycerides
  • Triggers atrial fibrillation ('holiday heart')
  • Heavy drinking weakens the heart muscle
  • Interacts with blood thinners and other medicines

Sleep, stress and the heart

Short sleep, loud snoring with pauses (sleep apnoea) and chronic stress all raise blood pressure and heart risk.

What helps

  • 7–8 hours of sleep and a regular schedule
  • Getting checked for sleep apnoea if you snore and feel sleepy by day
  • Yoga, breathing practice and time outdoors

Diabetes and the heart

Diabetes more than doubles the risk of heart disease. Heart protection is as important as sugar control.

Protect your heart

  • HbA1c target agreed with your doctor (often below 7%)
  • Blood pressure and LDL treated to strict targets
  • Newer diabetes medicines (SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists) that also protect the heart and kidneys
  • Yearly checks of kidneys, eyes and feet
